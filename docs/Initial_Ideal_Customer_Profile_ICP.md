# Initial Ideal Customer Profile (ICP)

*60-Day AI Revenue Sprint | Version 1.0*

| ICP in one sentence: 50–250 employee, knowledge-work-heavy companies whose leaders already believe AI matters and whose teams are using or experimenting with AI, but that have not yet translated adoption into coordinated end-to-end workflows and measurable bottom-line impact. |
| :---- |

# **1\. Company Size**

**•** Primary range: 50–250 employees.

**•** Strongest fit: organizations large enough to have repeated workflows, multiple handoffs, management layers, and meaningful labor economics—but still small enough that a focused transformation can move faster than a large-enterprise program.

**•** Companies outside the range are exceptions, not the default. Consider them only when the workflow economics, buyer access, urgency, and delivery fit are unusually strong.

# **2\. Types of Knowledge Work We Want to See**

The best prospects contain recurring work where people spend meaningful time finding, moving, interpreting, drafting, reviewing, reconciling, routing, or reporting information. Examples include:

**•** Operations and business-process coordination.

**•** Project/program management and status reporting.

**•** Customer support, service operations, and case handling.

**•** Sales operations, account research, proposal preparation, and CRM administration.

**•** Marketing/content operations and campaign production workflows.

**•** Finance and administrative workflows involving reconciliation, summaries, documentation, or approvals.

**•** HR/recruiting/onboarding workflows.

**•** Software, product, QA, and engineering-support workflows where AI/automation can reduce repetitive analysis, documentation, testing, or coordination.

**•** Executive/management reporting, meeting synthesis, decision support, and cross-functional information flow.

The goal is not to target a particular department first. The goal is to find knowledge-work environments where repeated manual effort is expensive enough that improving the workflow produces measurable economic value.

# **3\. AI-Readiness Signals**

A strong prospect should not need to be convinced that AI exists or matters. Positive signals include:

**•** Employees already use tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, Gemini, or AI features embedded in business software.

**•** Leadership publicly discusses AI, productivity, automation, digital transformation, or efficiency.

**•** The company is hiring for AI, automation, transformation, data, innovation, or adjacent roles.

**•** Different teams have run pilots or experiments, even if adoption is fragmented.

**•** The company has purchased AI-capable software but cannot clearly explain the economic return.

**•** Managers know there is repetitive work that should be easier but do not have a clear end-to-end owner for redesigning it.

**•** There is enough technical openness, data access, and organizational willingness to evaluate a practical workflow change.

# **4\. Manual Workflow Characteristics**

We are looking for workflows with several of the following characteristics:

**•** Repeated frequently (daily, weekly, or at meaningful monthly volume).

**•** Consumes material employee or management time.

**•** Touches multiple people, teams, systems, or handoffs.

**•** Requires people to copy, summarize, search, classify, compare, draft, reconcile, route, or re-enter information.

**•** Produces bottlenecks, delays, backlogs, inconsistent quality, or avoidable rework.

**•** Depends on tribal knowledge or a few key people to keep it moving.

**•** Contains decision points where AI can assist but human review can remain where judgment/risk requires it.

**•** Has a baseline that can be estimated or measured: hours, labor cost, cycle time, throughput, errors, rework, or lost capacity.

**•** Is painful enough that leadership would care if the process could materially improve.

# **5\. What “Operationally Immature” Means Here**

Operationally immature does not mean the company is poorly run. It means its AI adoption has advanced faster than its operating model. Common signs include:

**•** AI use is individual rather than systemic: a few employees have strong personal workflows, but the company has not redesigned the process around them.

**•** Teams use different tools and prompts with little standardization or governance.

**•** AI is layered onto existing work instead of eliminating steps, handoffs, duplicate effort, or waiting time.

**•** There is no clear owner accountable for converting AI experimentation into measurable workflow ROI.

**•** The company cannot quantify what its AI investment is saving or producing.

**•** Employees still move information manually between systems even though AI/automation could coordinate part of the workflow.

**•** Pilots exist, but there is no repeatable method for prioritizing which workflows should be transformed first.

**•** Leadership enthusiasm is ahead of implementation discipline.

**•** Security, quality, human-review, and change-management questions have not been translated into a practical operating approach.

| The “messy middle” is the target: not AI-resistant and not AI-optimized. The company has enough belief and activity to move, but enough fragmentation and unrealized value for us to create meaningful ROI. |

# **6\. Likely Buyer / Champion Roles**

The exact title will vary. We are looking for people who own a costly workflow, control transformation budget, or can sponsor access to the right team. Likely roles include:

**•** CEO / President / Founder (especially in the lower end of the size range).

**•** COO / Head of Operations / VP Operations.

**•** CIO / CTO / Head of Technology / Digital Transformation leader.

**•** Chief of Staff / Strategy / Transformation / Innovation leader.

**•** VP or Director of a function with a specific workflow problem (Customer Operations, Sales Operations, Finance, HR, Marketing, Product, Engineering, etc.).

**•** Program/PMO leader responsible for cross-functional transformation.

**•** A credible internal AI champion with access to the actual decision-maker and workflow owner.

# **7\. Strong-Fit Signals**

**•** Leadership wants AI to produce measurable business outcomes, not merely employee experimentation.

**•** A real workflow problem can be named before or during the Diagnostic.

**•** The workflow involves enough people/time/frequency for improvement to matter economically.

**•** The prospect can reach or influence the workflow owner and buyer.

**•** The company is willing to provide enough process and technical context to evaluate feasibility.

**•** A pilot can be scoped tightly enough to produce proof in weeks, not an open-ended transformation program.

**•** The result could plausibly repeat in similar companies or adjacent workflows.

# **8\. Disqualifiers / Poor-Fit Signals**

**•** The company is primarily looking for generic AI training, inspirational speaking, or a list of tools rather than workflow transformation.

**•** No meaningful workflow pain can be identified, or the problem is too small to justify founder time.

**•** The prospect has no decision influence and no realistic path to the workflow owner or buyer.

**•** Leadership is fundamentally resistant to AI and expects us to create organizational belief from zero.

**•** The organization is already highly AI-optimized with a mature internal transformation team and little material gap we can improve.

**•** The opportunity requires enterprise-scale architecture, mission-critical infrastructure, highly specialized regulated implementation, or security/compliance commitments beyond our proven delivery scope.

**•** The company expects a large amount of unpaid solution design or implementation before committing commercially.

**•** Data/system access is impossible or the technical environment makes a meaningful pilot unrealistic.

**•** The workflow is so custom or technically complex that proof would take too long for an early engagement.

**•** Budget, urgency, or ownership is absent and there is no credible event likely to change that soon.

# **9\. Prospect Classification**

| Classification | Definition | Action |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Strong Fit | Meets company-size/knowledge-work profile, is AI-active, has meaningful workflow pain, credible buyer access, and plausible economic/technical opportunity. | Advance through qualification and, if the criteria are met, invite to the Diagnostic. |
| Possible Fit | Some indicators are strong, but one or more important facts are unknown or borderline. | Request only the missing information or hold for review; do not spend Diagnostic time until minimum criteria are met. |
| Poor Fit | Misses critical market, pain, access, readiness, economics, or feasibility requirements. | Provide useful assessment where appropriate, but do not schedule the Diagnostic. |

# **10\. ICP Quick Test**

| A prospect is close to the center of the ICP when we can say “yes” to most of these: They are 50–250 employees; knowledge work is central to operations; AI is already being used; adoption is fragmented; a repeated workflow is expensive or painful; leadership wants measurable impact; a buyer/champion is accessible; and a scoped implementation could create visible proof quickly. |
| :---- |

# **Completion Standard**

This ICP is complete when either founder can review a company and consistently classify it as strong fit, possible fit, or poor fit using the company profile, AI-readiness signals, workflow characteristics, buyer access, and disqualifiers above.
